From a research question to a protocol and international collaboration

A research protocol gives a broad question a method, defines the evidence to collect and makes collaboration possible.

By Guilherme Menezes Pereira · Editorial date 1 November 2026

Abridged English edition · Portuguese original published 6 November 2026

Learning to frame a research question

In an undergraduate project, curiosity is an excellent starting point. Turning it into research takes another step. Which observation would help answer the question, and can the student actually make it with the time, supervision and equipment available?

Choosing a question that fits

Consider a hypothetical study of thermal comfort in classrooms. Improving comfort in schools is a worthwhile aim. An investigation needs a narrower question that identifies the rooms, times, variables and comparison criteria. This is an example of study design. I have no findings from such a study to report here.

Writing the protocol

Before collecting data, set out the inclusion criteria, instruments, measurement procedure and approach to analysis. Record how missing observations will be handled and keep dated versions when the method changes. That record lets another reader understand which decisions preceded the results.

Reproducing an analysis

The National Academies’ 2019 report distinguishes computational reproducibility, which uses the same data and analytical steps, from replication, in which another study investigates the same question with its own data. State which meaning applies when discussing the reliability of an analysis.

Giving data context

A spreadsheet becomes reusable when each field has a definition, unit, source and explanation of missing values. The FAIR principles also address identifiers, provenance and access conditions. Responsible access can require authentication and permission.

Finding international collaborators

A useful first message explains the question, identifies work by the prospective partner that helped shape it and describes a contribution the student could make. A specific request for advice on the method gives the recipient something concrete to respond to. International participation can begin with that exchange.

Agreeing on responsibilities

Agree who will collect and curate data, analyse it and draft the paper. Set review dates. CRediT describes fourteen types of contribution. Authorship decisions still follow the relevant institutional and journal criteria. The Singapore Statement also calls for honesty, accountability and fairness between collaborators.

Leaving a usable record

A small first project can leave a protocol, documented data and an account of the decisions behind the analysis. Another student should be able to identify the question, follow the method and see which findings need further investigation.
