From listening to a technical question: what community engagement teaches civil engineers

Community projects begin by turning everyday experiences into engineering questions that can be investigated responsibly.

By Guilherme Menezes Pereira · Editorial date 18 October 2026

Abridged English edition · Portuguese original published 6 November 2026

In this article

  2. Distinguish accounts, observations and interpretations
  3. A hypothetical public square
  4. Ask whose voice is missing
  5. Return findings in a usable form
  6. Evaluate what the project achieved

  8. References

Let the community help define the question

A resident may describe a difficult route, an uncomfortable square or a room that feels hot. These accounts identify problems worth exploring. Brazilian guidance on university extension connects teaching and research with dialogue between universities and society. People who use a space should be able to challenge the team's initial framing.

Distinguish accounts, observations and interpretations

A resident avoiding a path after rain describes an experience. Water photographed at a particular point and time is an observation. An explanation of why it accumulated is an interpretation requiring investigation. Record each accurately and identify where specialist assessment is needed.

A hypothetical public square

Suppose a community association asks a university team to study a square that families find hard to use. Conversations reveal uncomfortable seating, conflicts between play and circulation, and a sense of insecurity late in the day. A feasible study might map uses and barriers at agreed times. Two days of observation cannot represent an entire year.

Ask whose voice is missing

A meeting time, an online-only questionnaire or a personal invitation can change who participates. Record how participants were invited, who could not be reached and what the sample supports. The UN-Habitat assessment guide connects observations of a site with the experience of its users. Privacy and institutional guidance also shape how records can be shared.

Return findings in a usable form

An accessible map, a short account of priorities and a list of questions needing further study can help a community use the work. Invite participants to identify factual errors in the map. Agree on a readable format and who will receive it.

Evaluate what the project achieved

Counting meetings and documents describes activity. Learning may be visible when a student revises an assumption or explains a limitation. A partner can say whether the material supports a decision. At the end of the semester, record outstanding questions and any follow-up that was actually agreed.

Engineering begins before calculation

Listening carefully, defining scope and returning a defensible finding are technical skills. A modest conclusion that people can inspect is more useful than a design proposed before the team understands the problem.
