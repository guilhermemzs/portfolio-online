Technical English in engineering: communicating decisions precisely

Engineering communication depends on preserving meaning across languages, including dimensions, revisions, units and deadlines.

By Guilherme Menezes Pereira · Editorial date 25 October 2026

Abridged English edition · Portuguese original published 6 November 2026

In this article
  1. Start with the task
  2. Read the scope of the claim
  3. Keep context in a glossary
  4. Make a request someone can answer
  5. Check quantities and dates
  6. Review digital assistance
  7. Practise with real deliverables
  8. References

Start with the task

“Please check the dimensions” is grammatically correct. The recipient still needs the drawing revision, dimensions to check, purpose and deadline. Reading a research paper, discussing a clash and explaining a result to a client each call for a different vocabulary and level of detail.

Read the scope of the claim

Look for the material tested, test conditions, comparison and limitations. Words such as “may” and “under these conditions” qualify a claim. Dropping them in translation can turn a plausible finding into a promise the authors never made.

Keep context in a glossary

Record a term alongside its definition, source and an example from the discipline. The same word can have different meanings in a drawing, contract and laboratory report. A bilingual glossary becomes more useful when a reader can tell which meaning belongs to each context.

Make a request someone can answer

Identify the document and revision, describe the uncertainty and say what response is needed. If confirmation matters for the next task, ask the recipient to state the value or decision explicitly. This also leaves a record for colleagues outside the original conversation.

Check quantities and dates

Review the physical quantity, unit and conversion separately from the grammar. A date such as 04/05/2026 has two plausible readings. Spelling out the month and specifying a time zone for deadlines removes ambiguity. JPL’s preliminary findings on the loss of Mars Climate Orbiter illustrate why teams must verify units at system interfaces.

Review digital assistance

Translation and writing tools can suggest wording. Compare their output with the source for changes to numbers, negation, obligations, units and scope. Keep the original passage available when a technical decision depends on its interpretation.

Practise with real deliverables

A short request for clarification, an annotated extract or a summary of a method makes a useful exercise. Ask a second reader what they would do next. Their answer reveals whether the message gave them enough information to act.
